## What cardiac arrest is
1. Cardiac arrest occurs when the heart malfunctions and stops beating unexpectedly; it is an
   electrical problem. [A1]
2. A heart attack occurs when blood flow to the heart is blocked; it is a circulation problem. [A1]
3. Cardiac arrest is the only emergency in which the actions of the nearest person in the first
   three minutes matter more than everything done by everyone else afterwards. [H]
4. The rhythms of cardiac arrest: ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia
   are shockable; pulseless electrical activity and asystole are not shockable — they are treated
   with CPR and the search for reversible causes. [A4]
5. In cardiac arrest the heart produces no effective cardiac output: no pulse, no normal
   breathing, unresponsive. Chest compressions generate blood flow until the heart is restarted. [A4]

## The scale of the problem
6. Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest: approximately 350,000 people a year in the United States; it
   results in death about 90% of the time. [A1]
7. Survival to hospital discharge after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest is approximately 10.5%;
   21.1% when the arrest happens in a public place, 8.9% at home. [A2]
8. In-hospital cardiac arrest survival is approximately 23.6% in adults and 45.2% in children;
   79.2% of adult survivors have a favourable neurological outcome at discharge. [A2]
9. Only about 41% of adults with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest receive bystander CPR before
   emergency services arrive (41.7% in the executive summary). [A1, A2]
10. CPR given immediately can double or even triple the chance of survival. [A1]
11. Survival and neurological recovery are worse in minoritized racial and ethnic groups and in
    rural areas. [A2, A3]

## The 2025 unified Chain of Survival
12. The 2025 guidelines replace the four separate 2020 chains (adult and paediatric, in-hospital
    and out-of-hospital) with a single unified Chain of Survival for adults and children, in
    hospital and out of hospital. [H, A3]
13. The six links, in order: early recognition and emergency activation; high-quality CPR;
    defibrillation; advanced resuscitation; post–cardiac arrest care; recovery and survivorship. [A3]
14. The CPR link's symbol now includes the lungs alongside chest compressions — breathing
    matters, especially in children and in opioid-related arrest. [A3, A1]
15. Timely defibrillation remains a cornerstone of cardiac arrest care. [A3]
16. The details of each link vary between settings, but the chain is the same. [A3]

## What does not change in 2025 (handbook)
17. Compression rate 100 to 120 per minute; 30:2 ratio for single-rescuer adult CPR; the
    reversible causes and the overall shape of the algorithms still hold. [H]
18. Terminology: "rescue breaths" is no longer used; "breaths" means assisted breathing;
    "ventilation" is reserved for mechanical respiratory support. [H]

## Textbook science added for explanatory depth (owner policy 2026-10-03)
Standard physiology used to explain the mechanisms above; not client-source statements.
Each is uncontroversial textbook content. **Logged for clinical review.**
- [T1] Each heartbeat starts as an electrical impulse that spreads through the heart muscle so
  the chambers contract in sequence (atria, then ventricles). — Guyton & Hall, Textbook of
  Medical Physiology, ch. 10 (rhythmical excitation of the heart).
- [T2] A normal resting heart rate is about 60 to 100 beats a minute; about seventy is typical. — Guyton & Hall ch. 9.
- [T3] In ventricular fibrillation, muscle fibres depolarise out of step, the ventricles quiver
  instead of contracting, and no blood is ejected (cardiac output falls to zero); the pulse
  is lost within seconds. — Guyton & Hall ch. 13 (cardiac arrhythmias).
- [T4] Without cerebral blood flow the patient loses consciousness within seconds; occasional
  gasping (agonal breathing) is not normal breathing. — AHA BLS Provider Manual (recognition of arrest).
- [T5] In a heart attack (myocardial infarction) a coronary artery is blocked and part of the heart
  muscle is starved of blood; the patient is usually conscious, often with chest pain; a heart
  attack can trigger cardiac arrest. — AHA "Heart attack and sudden cardiac arrest differences".
- [T6] Pulseless ventricular tachycardia: the ventricles beat so fast they cannot fill, so there is no
  pulse. Pulseless electrical activity: organised electrical activity without a pulse.
  Asystole: no electrical activity. — AHA ACLS Provider Manual (cardiac arrest rhythms).
- [T7] A defibrillation shock depolarises the myocardium at once, allowing the heart's own
  pacemaker to restore an organised rhythm; it cannot help when there is no electrical activity
  (asystole) or the rhythm is already organised (PEA). — AHA ACLS Provider Manual.
- [T8] Chest compressions squeeze the heart between the sternum and the spine and raise pressure
  in the chest, producing blood flow to the brain and the heart muscle. — AHA BLS Provider Manual.
- [T9] Every minute without compressions or defibrillation lowers the chance of survival. — AHA BLS Provider Manual.
- [T10] Arrests in a public place are more likely to be witnessed and receive bystander CPR; in-hospital
  arrests are usually witnessed with help close by — the explanation for the survival differences in fact 7–8.
