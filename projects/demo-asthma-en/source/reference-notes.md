# Reference notes — Asthma: What Happens in the Airways

Fact ledger for AS01. Only well-established facts; each cited. No numbers unless certain.

## What asthma is
- Asthma is a chronic (long-term) disease of the airways, characterised by chronic inflammation
  of the airway wall. [GINA 2024 Global Strategy for Asthma Management and Prevention, ch.1
  "Definition of asthma"]
- It is defined by a history of respiratory symptoms (wheeze, shortness of breath, chest
  tightness, cough) that vary over time and in intensity, together with variable expiratory
  airflow limitation. [GINA 2024, ch.1 "Definition of asthma"]
- Asthma is a common condition with no cure, but symptoms can usually be controlled.
  [NHS, "Asthma" — Overview]

## Where it happens
- Asthma affects the bronchi and smaller airways (bronchioles) inside the lungs, the tubes that
  carry air in and out. [NHS, "Asthma" — Overview; WHO, "Asthma" fact sheet — Overview]

## What happens in an attack (the mechanism)
- In people with asthma the airways are inflamed and become sensitive, reacting strongly to
  things that don't bother other people's airways ("triggers"). [NHS, "Asthma" — Causes]
- When the airways react to a trigger, three things narrow the airway together: the muscle in
  the airway wall tightens (bronchoconstriction), the airway lining becomes swollen and
  inflamed, and the airway produces extra, thicker mucus. [GINA 2024, ch.1 "Definition of
  asthma" / "Pathophysiology"; NHS, "Asthma" — Causes]
- This narrowing of the airway makes it harder for air to move in and out, producing asthma
  symptoms. [GINA 2024, ch.1 "Pathophysiology"; NHS, "Asthma" — Symptoms]
- An asthma attack (exacerbation) is an acute or subacute worsening of these symptoms and lung
  function from the person's usual state. [GINA 2024, ch.1 "Definition of asthma exacerbations"]

## Common symptoms
- The main symptoms are wheezing, shortness of breath (breathlessness), a tight feeling in the
  chest, and coughing. [NHS, "Asthma" — Symptoms; WHO, "Asthma" fact sheet — Symptoms]
- Symptoms can vary over time and in intensity, and are often worse at night or first thing in
  the morning, or brought on by exercise or cold air. [GINA 2024, ch.1 "Definition of asthma";
  NHS, "Asthma" — Symptoms]

## Common triggers
- Recognised asthma triggers include allergens (such as pollen, dust mites, and animal fur or
  hair), tobacco smoke, cold air, exercise, and respiratory (viral) infections. [NHS, "Asthma" —
  Causes; WHO, "Asthma" fact sheet — Risk factors]
- Different people react to different triggers, and a trigger for one person may not affect
  another. [NHS, "Asthma" — Causes]

## Inhalers — the two main kinds
- Reliever inhalers are taken to relieve asthma symptoms quickly, by relaxing the tightened
  muscles around the narrowed airway; they act rapidly but do not treat the underlying
  inflammation. [NHS, "Asthma" — Treatment; GINA 2024, ch.3 "Reliever medications"]
- Preventer inhalers are taken regularly (usually daily) to reduce inflammation in the airways
  over time and prevent symptoms and attacks from happening in the first place; they must be
  used every day, even when feeling well, to work. [NHS, "Asthma" — Treatment; GINA 2024, ch.3
  "Controller medications"]

## A common mistake
- Overreliance on reliever inhalers, with underuse of the prescribed preventer, is associated
  with poorer asthma control and higher risk of attacks; people are advised not to stop or skip
  their preventer just because they feel well. [GINA 2024, ch.3 "Treatment of modifiable risk
  factors" / "Adherence"; NHS, "Asthma" — Treatment]
- Incorrect inhaler technique is common and reduces the amount of medicine that reaches the
  airways, so checking technique is part of routine asthma review. [GINA 2024, ch.3 "Inhaler
  skills"; NHS, "Asthma" — Treatment]
