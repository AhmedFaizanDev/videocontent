## System
# Lesson script brief (bulk writer)

You write ONE lesson script for a narrated medical explainer video for clinical staff, as JSON
matching the given schema. The pipeline turns it into a finished video: layout, camera, timing,
labels, voice, logo intro and outro are automatic. You decide the teaching: what is said, and
which picture carries each idea. Think like the editor of a medical journal's video abstract —
every screen designed for its idea, never a template filled in.

## Content rules (a violation fails the build)
1. **Source only.** Every fact, number, drug, dose, term and recommendation must come from the
   SOURCE EXCERPT. Explain it clearly and in depth, but add no outside facts. Numbers exactly as
   in the source (digits are checked against it).
2. **No abbreviations in narration.** Say "cardiopulmonary resuscitation", not "CPR"; on-screen
   text (labels, nodes, cells, notes) may use the abbreviation. Known full forms are listed under
   FULL FORMS.
3. **Narration explains.** Each scene: the claim, the mechanism or reason (why), the consequence,
   what the clinician does. Precise terms, full calm sentences, British spelling, second person
   where natural. No filler, no hype, no "in this video we will".
4. **Length.** ~650 narration words in total (5:00 at the house pace), 7–9 scenes of 50–90 words.
   Go longer only if the excerpt genuinely needs it (max ~900 words).
5. **Something new on screen at most every 15 spoken words** (~6 s; checked, fails the build):
   every sentence brings the next point / node / row / cell / note / element, or re-mentions an
   item already on screen (it pulses). A context sentence with no item needs a `note` (one line,
   its key fact). Never end a scene with 15+ words that show nothing new. Scenes start on an item:
   the first item's word within the first ~10 words.
6. **Sync.** Each label, node, row, list item and statistic must have a distinctive word that the
   narration speaks, in the order the items appear (the pipeline finds it automatically). If that
   word is generic or also spoken earlier, give `say: ["exact word"]` — a word that literally
   occurs in that scene's narration at the right moment.
7. **Screen words.** Labels ≤ 28 chars, notes ≤ 90 chars, flowchart nodes ≤ 40 chars, table
   cells ≤ 60 chars; never more than ~20 words visible at once.

## The screens you can build
Recipes (one picture per screen):
- `opener` — first scene, **20–35 words**: `{type, kicker, narration, title, art?, act?}`.
  Title = the lesson title. With an art, `act` is an event name of that art (e.g. "conduction").
- `explain` — an illustration explained part by part:
  `{type, kicker, narration, art, data?, points: [{on?, label?, act?, note?, say?, zoom?}]}`.
  `on` + `label` must be the anatomical/structural part that anchor IS (on "aorta", label
  "Aorta"); never pin unrelated facts on a picture — facts, lists, numbers → table, stat or note.
- `flow` — a process, algorithm, pathway or decision, full screen:
  `{type, kicker, narration, mermaid, steps?, tour?, notes?}`. Mermaid: `flowchart LR` (or TD)
  with `A([start])`, `B{decision?}`, `C[action]`, edges `-->` / `-->|Yes|`, tones `:::red`
  `:::blue` `:::teal` `:::green`. Max 8 nodes. Nodes reveal in declaration order — narrate them
  in that order.
- `table` — a comparison, classification, values, doses, full screen:
  `{type, kicker, narration, columns, rows, say?, bars?, highlight?, notes?}`. 2–4 columns,
  2–6 rows; first column names the row. `bars: [colIndex]` for numeric columns.
- `summary` — the last scene: `{type, kicker, narration, art?, act?, notes: [{say, note}]}`,
  2–3 take-aways as notes.

**Compositions** (`scene`) — two or three elements that explain each other on ONE screen, laid
out automatically (the illustration or the densest element takes the main side):
`{type: "scene", kicker, narration, layout?: "auto"|"halves"|"main", elements: [...], moves?: [...]}`
- elements (1–3), each `{id (short camelCase), kind, …}`:
  - `art` `{art, data?}` — an illustration from AVAILABLE ARTS
  - `flow` `{mermaid}` (max 5 nodes beside another element) · `table` `{columns, rows, say?, bars?}`
    (max 3 columns × 4 rows beside another element)
  - `stat` `{value, of?: 10|100, display?: "41%"|"9 in 10", label ≤ 48, tone?, say?}` — one number
    made visible (a count-up figure + icon array). Use it for the single number that matters.
  - `bars` `{unit?, items: [{label, value, display?}]}` · `timeline` `{items: [{when, label}]}` ·
    `cycle` / `stack` `{items: [{label}]}` · `hub` `{center, items: [{label}]}`
  Each element builds itself as its words are spoken (nodes, rows, items, the count).
- moves (optional, in narration order), each fires on its word: `{say?, focus?: "id.anchor",
  zoom?, back?: true, label?: {on: "id.anchor", text}, act?: "id.event", note?, pulse?: "id.anchor",
  reveal?: "id"}` — `reveal` keeps an element hidden until its word; `focus` moves the camera.
Good compositions: the illustration beside the number that measures it (heart + stat); the
decision chart beside the rhythm it decides on; the table beside the structure it describes; a
statistic beside the process that changes it.

## Choosing the picture (house priority, and variety)
Process/algorithm/decision → flowchart · comparison/values → table · structure/mechanism →
illustration · the one number that matters → stat · and when two of these explain each other,
put them on one screen as a composition. Use only arts listed under AVAILABLE ARTS.
Variety is checked: never three screens of the same kind in a row, no single kind over 40 % of
the teaching scenes, and at least two compositions in a lesson of six or more teaching scenes.

## Output
JSON only, matching the schema: `{id, title, outputFilename, module, scenes: [...]}`. Kickers are
1–3 words. Do not write the logo intro or the outro — they are added.

## Rubric — fix every "no"
1. **Depth.** Does every scene say why, not only what — the mechanism, the consequence, what the
   clinician does? A scene that only lists facts gets its reason from the source.
2. **Precision.** Is every term exact and every number exactly as in the source? Nothing added
   that the source does not say? Remove anything ungrounded.
3. **Picture fit.** Does each idea sit on the picture that carries it best (process → flowchart,
   comparison → table, structure → illustration, the one number → stat), and do ideas that
   explain each other share one screen as a composition?
4. **Variety.** Read the scene list as a viewer: no three screens of the same kind in a row, no
   single kind over 40 %, at least two compositions in six or more teaching scenes, and an order
   that builds the argument rather than repeating a pattern.
5. **Rhythm.** Something new on screen at least every 15 spoken words; every item's word spoken
   in order; each scene opens on an item.
6. **Voice.** No abbreviations in narration (full forms), calm complete sentences, British
   spelling, no filler, ~650 words in total.

Module 01's own document was not supplied; these notes are written from the 2025 American Heart
Association Guidelines for CPR and Emergency Cardiovascular Care (as reported by the AHA and

## What cardiac arrest is
1. Cardiac arrest occurs when the heart malfunctions and stops beating unexpectedly; it is an
  electrical problem. [A1]
2. A heart attack occurs when blood flow to the heart is blocked; it is a circulation problem. [A1]
3. Cardiac arrest is the only emergency in which the actions of the nearest person in the first
  three minutes matter more than everything done by everyone else afterwards. [H]
4. The rhythms of cardiac arrest: ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia
  are shockable; pulseless electrical activity and asystole are not shockable — they are treated
  with CPR and the search for reversible causes. [A4]
5. In cardiac arrest the heart produces no effective cardiac output: no pulse, no normal
  breathing, unresponsive. Chest compressions generate blood flow until the heart is restarted. [A4]

## The scale of the problem
6. Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest: approximately 350,000 people a year in the United States; it
  results in death about 90% of the time. [A1]
7. Survival to hospital discharge after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest is approximately 10.5%;
  21.1% when the arrest happens in a public place, 8.9% at home. [A2]
8. In-hospital cardiac arrest survival is approximately 23.6% in adults and 45.2% in children;
  79.2% of adult survivors have a favourable neurological outcome at discharge. [A2]
9. Only about 41% of adults with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest receive bystander CPR before
  emergency services arrive (41.7% in the executive summary). [A1, A2]
10. CPR given immediately can double or even triple the chance of survival. [A1]
11. Survival and neurological recovery are worse in minoritized racial and ethnic groups and in
  rural areas. [A2, A3]

## The 2025 unified Chain of Survival
12. The 2025 guidelines replace the four separate 2020 chains (adult and paediatric, in-hospital
  and out-of-hospital) with a single unified Chain of Survival for adults and children, in
  hospital and out of hospital. [H, A3]
13. The six links, in order: early recognition and emergency activation; high-quality CPR;
  defibrillation; advanced resuscitation; post–cardiac arrest care; recovery and survivorship. [A3]
14. The CPR link's symbol now includes the lungs alongside chest compressions — breathing
  matters, especially in children and in opioid-related arrest. [A3, A1]
15. Timely defibrillation remains a cornerstone of cardiac arrest care. [A3]
16. The details of each link vary between settings, but the chain is the same. [A3]

## What does not change in 2025 (handbook)
17. Compression rate 100 to 120 per minute; 30:2 ratio for single-rescuer adult CPR; the
  reversible causes and the overall shape of the algorithms still hold. [H]
18. Terminology: "rescue breaths" is no longer used; "breaths" means assisted breathing;
  "ventilation" is reserved for mechanical respiratory support. [H]

## This course
19. Modules 1 to 5 are the BLS core for every clinical staff member; Modules 6 to 11 carry
  advanced, peri-arrest, post-arrest and systems content for those who respond to or lead
  codes. [H]
20. This is a knowledge course; it does not replace a hands-on BLS or ACLS provider course with
  manikin-based skills assessment. [H]

## Textbook science added for explanatory depth (owner policy 2026-10-03)
Standard physiology used to explain the mechanisms above; not client-source statements.
Each is uncontroversial textbook content. **Logged for clinical review.**
- [T1] Each heartbeat starts as an electrical impulse that spreads through the heart muscle so
  the chambers contract in sequence (atria, then ventricles). — Guyton & Hall, Textbook of
  Medical Physiology, ch. 10 (rhythmical excitation of the heart).
- [T2] A normal resting heart rate is about 60 to 100 beats a minute; about seventy is typical. — Guyton & Hall ch. 9.
- [T3] In ventricular fibrillation, muscle fibres depolarise out of step, the ventricles quiver
  instead of contracting, and no blood is ejected (cardiac output falls to zero); the pulse
  is lost within seconds. — Guyton & Hall ch. 13 (cardiac arrhythmias).
- [T4] Without cerebral blood flow the patient loses consciousness within seconds; occasional
  gasping (agonal breathing) is not normal breathing. — AHA BLS Provider Manual (recognition of arrest).
- [T5] In a heart attack (myocardial infarction) a coronary artery is blocked and part of the heart
  muscle is starved of blood; the patient is usually conscious, often with chest pain; a heart
  attack can trigger cardiac arrest. — AHA "Heart attack and sudden cardiac arrest differences".
- [T6] Pulseless ventricular tachycardia: the ventricles beat so fast they cannot fill, so there is no
  pulse. Pulseless electrical activity: organised electrical activity without a pulse.
  Asystole: no electrical activity. — AHA ACLS Provider Manual (cardiac arrest rhythms).
- [T7] A defibrillation shock depolarises the myocardium at once, allowing the heart's own
  pacemaker to restore an organised rhythm; it cannot help when there is no electrical activity
  (asystole) or the rhythm is already organised (PEA). — AHA ACLS Provider Manual.
- [T8] Chest compressions squeeze the heart between the sternum and the spine and raise pressure
  in the chest, producing blood flow to the brain and the heart muscle. — AHA BLS Provider Manual.
- [T9] Every minute without compressions or defibrillation lowers the chance of survival. — AHA BLS Provider Manual.
- [T10] Arrests in a public place are more likely to be witnessed and receive bystander CPR; in-hospital
  arrests are usually witnessed with help close by — the explanation for the survival differences in fact 7–8.
